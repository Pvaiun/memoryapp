Four ways to look at a week

Same seven days throughout — the real week of Sunday 26 July, with the real items, the real theme colours, and the chip styling lifted straight out of styles.css. Three horizontal attempts, then the one I'd actually want to test. Static mock-ups: nothing here is wired up.

What a week actually buys
7×the vertical room per day, and nothing else

That table is the whole problem, and it's worth sitting with before looking at the mock-ups. A horizontal week and a month grid are both seven columns of the same phone. The chips can't get wider going month → week, so they can't get bigger — 8.5px type and word-boundary wrapping is already the floor. All a week wins is height: no +N more truncation, no 4-line clamp, and room to breathe vertically.

So the question each variant answers is: what do you spend that height on? A spends it on showing everything. B spends it on a time ruler. C refuses the premise and takes width back from the empty days.

The month grid with one week in it. Same columns, same chips, same code — every day just gets the full body height instead of a 78px cell. Nothing is truncated and nothing is hidden behind a tap.

It reads fine, and that's the trouble: it reads almost exactly like the week you already see in the month view. Scroll the current calendar so this week is at the top and you get the same seven columns with the same titles — only Sunday's fifth item is missing. The whole gain, on this week, is one chip and a bit of air.

Look at how much of the frame is empty. Thirteen items across seven days leaves two-thirds of the screen unused, and Saturday is a column of pure whitespace holding a "1".

  • 1Costs almost nothing. A second row renderer in the same scroller — the chips, colours, and sort order are already written.
  • 2Gains almost nothing. Same width, same type size, same words. On a quiet week it's a month view with more air.
Ambient recurrences stay off, exactly as they do today: the dishwasher, the recycling, and the garbage all carry show_on_calendar = 0, so a week view doesn't suddenly bury the grid in chores.

This is the mock-up that makes the argument for you. It's the shape everyone pictures when they hear "week view" — and against this data it collapses.

Ten of the week's thirteen items have no clock time at all, so they pile into the all-day band at the top and the ruler underneath holds three events across twelve hours and seven days. The band — the part that isn't the week view — is doing all the work, and it's the part squeezed into 60px.

That's not a rendering problem, it's the data model. clock() treats a noon-exact timestamp as "no clock time", because dates captured without a time anchor to local noon. A deadline is a claim on a day. A recurrence like therapy has a time; "hanging out with Kyle" never will. An hour grid is a precision instrument for data that mostly doesn't carry hours.

  • 1Empty by construction. 84 cells on screen, 3 of them occupied.
  • 2Demotes the real content. Deadlines and all-day events — the bulk of what's here — get the thinnest strip on the page.
  • 3Fights the room. Timed items would need overlap resolution, drag targets, a now-line — a lot of machinery to display three things.

Columns sized by what's in them. Sunday holds five things, so it takes 77px — nearly double a month cell — and its chips jump to 10px type, readable at a glance with no squinting. Saturday is empty, so it shrinks to a 15px spine that still says "Saturday, nothing". Tuesday, Wednesday and Friday sit in between at 50px and 9.5px type.

This is the only variant where a week view looks different from the month view, because it's the only one that stops accepting 1/7th as the answer. It's also the idea the previous mock-up round parked: Concept B, "weighted days" — flagged then as worth prototyping inside a focus week, where losing aligned weekday columns costs least. A week view is exactly that place. There is no month shape left to break.

The cost is honest: the columns no longer line up with anything. You can't compare Tuesday to Tuesday, and the week's shape shifts every time you add an item — a day can widen under your thumb. For a calendar that's a real loss. For answering "what does my week look like", the emphasis is the answer.

  • 1Legibility where it's needed. The busy day gets the pixels; the empty day stops charging rent for them.
  • 2Shape at a glance. The week's load is visible in the geometry before you read a word.
  • 3Unstable and unalignable. No weekday grid, and widths that move as items land.
Worth noticing in the frame above: the win is confined to Sunday. At 50px, Tuesday and Wednesday still break words mid-title — "Manulif/e", "Therap/y" — exactly as they do in the month grid today. Weighting buys one genuinely readable day per week and leaves the rest where they were.
Variant C · The following week

Where a multi-day event lands

Aug 2 – 8 MonthWeek
Cottage trip · Kyle, Ari, Molly › S2 Mon3 due Gaming room sign T4 Wed5 Nyissa's defense party T6 Fri7 Sat8

The cottage trip runs Friday to Sunday, so it crosses the week boundary. Bands already work this way in the month grid — .wk-band with a cont-r edge and a › to say "keeps going" — and they port over unchanged.

But note what the weighting did here. Friday and Saturday hold one event between them, and it's the widest thing on screen, because a band needs width to be a band. So the weighting rule can't just count items — a span has to claim the days it covers, or the trip gets squeezed into two narrow spines and turns back into a chip.

That's the sort of special case that makes the weighted layout more than a one-afternoon change. The equal-column version in Variant A has none of it: the band spans columns 6–7 and that's the end of the logic.

The server already emits one event entry per spanned day (items.ts:399); the month grid filters them out with spanItemIds and draws the band instead. Either week variant reuses that untouched.

Let each shape do the job it's good at. Seven columns can't hold words, but it can hold a shape — so the spine keeps the week's geometry in 40px and says only what 43px can say honestly: which days are loaded. Everything below it is full-width, where the words actually fit.

That row is the whole argument. The row spans the full 328px and the title itself gets 253px against the month grid's 43px — 5.9×, the one thing no seven-column layout can give you. "Conversation with Kayla about a new bed" clipped or broke mid-word in every variant above; here it's just a sentence, at 12.5px, on one line.

The whole week is 492px of content — thirteen items and seven day headers, all legible, nothing tapped. This 724px mock frame shows 440px of it, so on this phone it's one short flick; on any phone taller than about 780px it's a single screen. Either way the comparison holds: the same week is 43px chips and a +N more in the month view, and an all-day band over an empty ruler in Variant B.

And it's largely already built: this is the day-detail bottom sheet (CalendarView.tsx:309) run over seven days instead of one. Which also means rows come back to life — tap to open, check off a DO — something the month grid can't do at all, because there a whole day is a single button and the chips are inert.

  • 2No lens, no zoom. It honours round 4 — one legible size, everything on screen readable. The taper is in the data, not the camera.

Spans are the detail vertical wins outright. In a horizontal week a multi-day event is a band that needs width, breaks at the week boundary, and needs a › to say it continues — and in Variant C it distorted the whole layout, because a band can't live in a 15px column.

Stacked, it's just a bracket down the left of the days it covers. The trip is named once, the dates it owns are obvious, and Saturday and Sunday stay ordinary days that happen to be inside it. No continuation marker, no week-boundary special case, no width to fight for.

Where this lands

The seven-column week is the trap

All three horizontal attempts fail the same way, and it took building them to see it clearly: they each accept that a week must be seven columns, and seven columns on a phone is 43px of width. Everything after that is damage control. A shows everything and gains nothing. B applies an hour grid to data that has no hours — 10 of this week's 13 items are date-only — and renders three events across 84 cells. C fights back for width and wins exactly one readable day, at the cost of the weekday grid and a pile of new rules for spans and priority.

D drops the premise. The seven-column shape is kept where it's honest — a 40px spine that says which days are heavy — and the content moves to full width, where a title is a sentence instead of a chip. That's the 7.6× the other three can't reach, and it's why the entire week fits on one screen with nothing tapped.

It's also the cheapest of the four to build, which is the part I didn't expect. A, B and C all need new layout engines. D needs the day-detail sheet's row renderer pointed at seven days instead of one — and it inherits working rows for free, so items become tappable and DOs become checkable inside the calendar for the first time.

What I'd actually want to test, if this gets built: (1) does the spine earn its 40px, or is it decoration once the agenda is right below it — the honest test is to ship it and try deleting it; (2) does a continuously-scrolling agenda want to be called "Week" at all, or is the week just a divider rule in a list that runs forever; (3) whether it makes the month grid feel redundant for anything except finding a specific date.

One thing that hasn't changed: the Brain already writes "Next week's appointments" as a card, unprompted, and it was on today's map. If the only question is what does my week look like, that sentence may still beat every mock-up on this page. D is worth building if you want the calendar to be somewhere you can act, not just read — that's the part the prose card can't do.
